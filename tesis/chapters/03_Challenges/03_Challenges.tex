\chapter{Related Work \& Challenges}
\label{ch:challenges}

The setting of this thesis is a winged drone flying through a forest of obstacles. The drone is evaluated on two quantities: how far it travels and how much energy it spends along the way. Its body is described by a vector of morphological parameters. Its controller is a neural network that reads the drone's sensors and drives its actuators. The whole chapter is built on the nested problem of \eqref{eq:codesign-bilevel}, which the first section introduces. There, a body $\vect{m}$ and a controller $\vect{\theta}$ are evaluated together by a performance $F(\vect{m}, \vect{\theta})$, which measures the quality of the flight of the body $\vect{m}$ under the controller $\vect{\theta}$. The only way to obtain the value of $F$ is to simulate that flight.

The chapter has two parts. The first part is the section on related work. It reviews the previous works on the co-design of body and controller and on evolved plasticity, and it states the position of this thesis with respect to both. The second part is made of the three sections after it, which present three challenges. Each challenge limits what a search over bodies and controllers can find, and each one therefore shapes the method of this thesis. The first challenge is the sequential design challenge. When the body is designed by hand and only the controller is optimized, a better body is never even tried. The second challenge is the morphology optimization challenge. When one controller is kept fixed and only the body is optimized, the search prefers the bodies that suit that controller over the bodies with the greatest potential. The third challenge is the computational feasibility challenge. The direct remedy, which trains a new controller for every body the search visits, costs more than any search can afford. Together, the three challenges define the requirement that the rest of the thesis sets out to meet: a controller that adapts to the body it is given, at a cost small enough to fit inside the body search.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related-work}

This thesis sits at the meeting point of two lines of research that have so far been pursued separately: the joint optimization of a robot's body and of its controller, and the design of neural controllers whose weights keep changing during operation under rules that are themselves the object of optimization. This section reviews the works of each line that are closest to what is done here, singling out in each case the design choice that the present work makes differently, and closes by stating where it stands with respect to both.

\subsection{Co-Design of Morphology and Control}
\label{subsec:rw-codesign}

Designing a robot means choosing a body and a controller, and the two choices are not independent: the best controller depends on the body it drives, and the value of a body can only be judged through the best behaviour it affords. \emph{Co-design} makes this coupling explicit by optimizing both. Writing $\vect{m}$ for a morphology in a design space $\mathcal{M}$, $\vect{\theta}$ for the parameters of a controller and $F(\vect{m}, \vect{\theta})$ for the performance of the closed loop they form, obtained by simulating it, the problem is a nested one:
\begin{equation}
\vect{m}^{\star} = \arg\max_{\vect{m} \in \mathcal{M}} F\big(\vect{m}, \vect{\theta}^{\star}(\vect{m})\big), \qquad \vect{\theta}^{\star}(\vect{m}) = \arg\max_{\vect{\theta}} F(\vect{m}, \vect{\theta}).
\label{eq:codesign-bilevel}
\end{equation}
The formulation states the problem without solving it: $F$ is a black box, and evaluating the outer objective at a single candidate $\vect{m}$ requires solving, or approximating, the inner problem for that body. The works reviewed below differ mainly in how they pay for the inner level, and \eqref{eq:codesign-bilevel} is a convenient way to compare them.

The earliest approach collapsed the two levels into one. Sims \cite{sims1994evolving} evolved virtual creatures whose genome, a directed graph, encoded at the same time the segments of the body and the neural circuits embedded in each segment, obtaining swimmers, walkers and jumpers from a single genetic algorithm; Lipson and Pollack \cite{lipson2000automatic} applied the same idea to machines made of bars, actuators and artificial neurons, and fabricated the evolved designs by rapid prototyping. In both cases the pair $(\vect{m}, \vect{\theta})$ is a single individual and the inner problem is never solved explicitly: the controller of a body is whatever the mutations happened to pair with it.

Cheney et al.\ \cite{cheney2018scalable} identified why this becomes a problem as the design space grows. A controller is co-adapted to the body it evolved with, so a mutation of the body is almost always harmful in the short term, however promising the new body may be once a controller is newly adapted to it. Selection therefore removes morphological novelty faster than it can be exploited, and the search converges prematurely on a body found early. Their remedy, \emph{morphological innovation protection}, temporarily lifts selection pressure from individuals whose body has just changed, giving the controller time to catch up before the body is judged. The underlying principle carries over to every method in this section: a body can only be assessed fairly once a controller has been adapted to it.

Eiben and Hart \cite{eiben2020evolves} turned that principle into a design rule, arguing that every newborn body should go through a learning phase before its fitness is measured. Gupta et al.\ \cite{gupta2021embodied} showed what this buys at scale. In their Deep Evolutionary Reinforcement Learning framework, bodies are kinematic trees of cylinders connected by hinge joints, a space of the order of $10^{18}$ morphologies; an asynchronous tournament evolution mutates limbs and joints, and the controller of every new body is trained from scratch by PPO (\autoref{subsec:ppo}) on the body's own egocentric sensors for a fixed budget of interactions, with the fitness of the body being the reward reached at the end of training. Two findings are relevant here. Bodies evolved in harder environments, uneven terrain and manipulation rather than flat ground, perform better when transferred to new tasks, which the authors read as environmental complexity fostering morphological intelligence. And although nothing selects for it explicitly, the time a controller needs to reach a given performance halves within ten generations: evolution finds bodies that are easier to control, the morphological counterpart of the Baldwin effect. What makes these results possible is also what makes the method expensive: the inner problem of \eqref{eq:codesign-bilevel} is paid in full at every evaluation, with a complete reinforcement learning run per body and no inheritance of the controller, which required more than a thousand CPUs running in parallel.

The same division of labour has recently been applied to drones. Muff et al.\ \cite{muff2026hexacopters} evolve hexacopters in which each of the six arms is described by its length, two placement angles, two motor tilt angles and the spin direction of its rotor. A $(\mu+\lambda)$ evolution strategy with tournament selection acts on these morphological parameters, with repair operators that resolve collisions between arms and a static hovering test that discards unviable designs, while the controller of each morphology, a small feed-forward network, is learned from scratch by PPO on four waypoint-following tasks (circle, slalom, shuttle run and figure of eight). The evolved drones, with tilted rotors and asymmetric arms, outperform the conventional hexacopter on every task. The authors also introduce metrics for the learning process itself and find that evolved bodies reach their learning breakthrough earlier and converge to higher rewards, confirming the Baldwin-like effect of Gupta et al. As in that work, evolution owns the morphology and reinforcement learning owns the controller, and the cost of the inner level, several hundred million simulation steps per body, is what limits the search to under a thousand morphologies.

Bergonti et al.\ \cite{bergonti2024codesign}, in the laboratory where this thesis was carried out, address a morphing winged drone of the same family as the one used in the present work, a fixed-wing glider whose wings carry actuated joints. The outer level is NSGA-II (\autoref{subsec:nsga2}) over the wing geometry, the number and orientation of the morphing joints of each wing, the choice of servomotors and propulsion unit from commercial catalogues, and a scalar that weighs the two objectives, energy consumption and mission time over a suite of scenarios. The inner level is not a learned controller but a trajectory optimization: for every candidate design, a nonlinear program solved by direct multiple shooting computes the time series of joint torques and thrust that completes each scenario at minimum cost, subject to the multibody dynamics with aerodynamic forces, the actuator limits, the obstacles and the checkpoints. The co-designed drones cut energy by more than a third and mission time by a fifth or more with respect to the commercial glider they start from. The choice of an open-loop optimal trajectory as inner solution is what makes tens of thousands of inner evaluations affordable on CPUs, but it assumes a known model and no disturbances, and it leaves the closed-loop controller that would fly the real drone outside the optimization.

A different way to escape the cost of the inner level is to amortize it. Gupta et al.\ \cite{gupta2022metamorph} train a single Transformer policy, MetaMorph, on a large set of bodies at once, encoding each limb as a token that combines its proprioceptive readings with a description of its geometry and joints, so that the policy reads the morphology it is controlling as one more input modality. The result generalizes zero-shot to unseen variations of kinematics and dynamics and transfers to new bodies with little additional training. In the terms of \eqref{eq:codesign-bilevel}, $\vect{\theta}^{\star}(\vect{m})$ is replaced by a single $\vect{\theta}$ for a policy $\pi(a \mid o, \vect{m})$ conditioned on $\vect{m}$: the inner problem is paid once, for the whole design space, at the price of telling the controller which body it has.

\subsection{Evolved and Learned Plasticity}
\label{subsec:rw-plasticity}

The idea of letting evolution choose the learning rule rather than the weights, introduced in \autoref{subsec:evolved-plasticity}, also has a history in the laboratory where this work was done. Floreano and Urzelai \cite{floreano2000evolutionary} evolved controllers for a Khepera robot in which the genome specified, for each synapse, which of four Hebbian rules it obeyed and at what rate, while the weights themselves were drawn at random at the beginning of every life and shaped only by the rule. On a task that required a sequence of behaviours, these controllers not only learned it within a life but, once evolved in simulation, transferred to the physical robot, and to a larger robot with a different body, better than controllers with evolved fixed weights. The review by Soltoggio, Stanley and Risi \cite{soltoggio2018born} consolidates two decades of such EPANNs and draws from them one lesson that matters for the present work: plasticity is selected only when something varies, between or within lifetimes, that a fixed genome cannot anticipate. On a stationary task evolution finds it cheaper to write the solution into fixed weights, and a plastic mechanism that is never needed is at best neutral and often harmful. Neuromodulation, the gating of the learning rate by a dedicated signal, is the other recurring theme of that literature \cite{soltoggio2008evolutionary}.

Miconi, Stanley and Clune \cite{miconi2018differentiable} took the opposite route and made the plasticity differentiable. Each connection carries a fixed weight $W_{ij}$ and a Hebbian trace $H_{ij}$ whose contribution is scaled by a coefficient $\alpha_{ij}$:
\begin{equation}
y_i = \sigma\Big( \sum_j \big( W_{ij} + \alpha_{ij}\, H_{ij} \big)\, x_j \Big), \qquad H_{ij} \leftarrow (1-\eta)\, H_{ij} + \eta\, x_j\, y_i,
\label{eq:diffplast}
\end{equation}
and $\mat{W}$, $\mat{\alpha}$ and $\eta$ are all trained by gradient descent, backpropagating through the entire lifetime of the network, trace updates included. The decomposition is instructive: the weight is split into a part that is learned once and a part that is written by experience and forgotten geometrically, and $\mat{\alpha}$ decides, synapse by synapse, how much of the second part the network is allowed. Plastic networks trained this way outperformed non-plastic recurrent networks on a pattern memorization task and competed with dedicated architectures on few-shot image classification, and a later extension, Backpropamine \cite{miconi2019backpropamine}, replaced the fixed $\eta$ by a neuromodulatory signal computed by the network itself. The price of differentiability is that the lifetime must be differentiable: the base weights and the rule are co-adapted by the same gradient, and the method does not apply when the lifetime is a closed-loop interaction with a physics simulator, which is why the works below, and this thesis, return to evolution.

Najarro and Risi \cite{najarro2020meta} did exactly that, with the parametrization of \eqref{eq:abcd} extended by a learning rate per synapse, so that every connection is described by five coefficients, and a natural evolution strategy \cite{salimans2017evolution} searching them. The weights of the network are redrawn uniformly at random at the start of every episode and organize themselves under the rules within a few dozen time steps, without any reward signal during the lifetime. On a simulated quadruped this produced the result that has since become the reference for the approach: a Hebbian network evolved on the intact body and on one damaged leg keeps walking when a leg it has never seen damaged is removed, where a network with evolved fixed weights falls. The same experiment also shows the cost: on the intact body the fixed-weight network walks farther than the plastic one. Plasticity buys robustness to a change of body at a price on the nominal body, and the random initialization at each episode is what forces the rules to do the whole job of building the controller.

Two later works ask where and how many rules are needed. Pedersen and Risi \cite{pedersen2021evolving} evolve the rules of the same quadruped and periodically merge them by clustering, so that many synapses come to share one rule; the number of evolved coefficients drops by more than an order of magnitude and the smaller set generalizes better to unseen damage, a computational version of the genomic bottleneck. Ferigo et al.\ \cite{ferigo2023evolving} evolve one rule per synapse in the controllers of voxel-based soft robots, whose body deforms as it moves, and report that the Hebbian controllers match or exceed static ones in locomotion and adapt to damage that was never seen during evolution.

Van Diggelen et al.\ \cite{vandiggelen2025emergent} bring evolved Hebbian rules to swarm robotics. Each robot carries a small memoryless network with a rule of the form \eqref{eq:abcd} on every synapse and a fixed learning rate, and a single set of rule coefficients, evolved by CMA-ES (\autoref{subsec:cmaes}) on the performance of the whole swarm, is shared by all its members. At the beginning of each trial every robot draws its own weights at random; since each robot then experiences a different sensory history, identical rules drive the weights of different robots to different places, and heterogeneous roles, including collective behavioural switching, emerge from a homogeneous genome. The rules outperform multi-agent reinforcement learning baselines on standard benchmarks and transfer from simulation to ten physical robots with a loss of under one percent. Two features of this design recur throughout the plasticity literature reviewed here: the controller converges from a random initialization at every trial, and the same rule set is used by every agent regardless of its individual situation.

\subsection{Positioning of This Thesis}
\label{subsec:rw-positioning}

Read through \eqref{eq:codesign-bilevel}, the co-design works above pay for the inner level in one of three ways:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{in full}, with a reinforcement learning run per body \cite{gupta2021embodied, muff2026hexacopters};
    \item \emph{exactly solved}, but for an open-loop trajectory, which leaves the closed-loop controller out of the design \cite{bergonti2024codesign};
    \item \emph{once}, with a policy that must be told which body it controls \cite{gupta2022metamorph}.
\end{enumerate}
The plasticity works, for their part, all build the controller from random weights at every episode \cite{floreano2000evolutionary, najarro2020meta, ferigo2023evolving, vandiggelen2025emergent} or co-train the base weights and the rule by the same gradient \cite{miconi2018differentiable}; none places evolved rules on top of a policy that was pretrained by reinforcement learning and then frozen, and none uses plasticity across a population of bodies inside a co-design loop.

This thesis works in the intersection of the two gaps. Its controller is a policy pretrained by reinforcement learning and then frozen, made plastic only at its output layer by evolved rules of the form \eqref{eq:abcd}, so that the plastic part is, as in \eqref{eq:diffplast}, a departure from a fixed part, except that the departure starts from trained weights rather than from random ones; and it is evaluated across a population of bodies inside a morphology search rather than on a single body. Why each of these choices is needed is the subject of the three challenges that follow, and how they are realized of \autoref{ch:method}.

\section{The Sequential Design Challenge}
\label{sec:challenge-sequential}

Usually, the design of a flying robot starts from the body. An engineer designs the body, or a commercial product provides it. A controller is then optimized for that body. This thesis trains the controller by reinforcement learning; other works tune it by hand or compute it by trajectory optimization. With respect to \eqref{eq:codesign-bilevel}, this procedure corresponds to fixing $\vect{m}$ at a chosen body $\vect{m}_0$ and solving only the inner problem, which returns $\vect{\theta}^{\star}(\vect{m}_0)$. The result is the best controller for the body $\vect{m}_0$. The best controller for one body is not the same thing as the best pair of body and controller. Any body $\vect{m}$ with $F(\vect{m}, \vect{\theta}^{\star}(\vect{m})) > F(\vect{m}_0, \vect{\theta}^{\star}(\vect{m}_0))$ would be a better choice than $\vect{m}_0$, and the sequential procedure never tries it.

Such better bodies exist, because the body design constrains what the controller can do. For example, the energy spent per metre of flight has a lower bound. This bound is set by the airframe, by its lift-to-drag ratio and by the mass it carries, and no controller can change it during flight. Likewise, the manoeuvrability of the drone has an upper bound. This bound is set by the control authority, that is, by the forces and moments the actuators can produce, and no controller can exceed it. The task of this thesis rewards two things: the distance travelled through a cluttered forest and the energy efficiency of the flight. A task that rewards both distance and efficiency asks for a trade-off between the two objectives. A single hand-designed body sits at one point of that trade-off, and that point was chosen before the task was specified. Other bodies may be better on distance, better on efficiency, or better on both objectives at once. Co-design studies report exactly this outcome: when every body is given its own controller, the evolved bodies outperform the conventional design they started from \cite{gupta2021embodied, muff2026hexacopters, bergonti2024codesign}, and the conventional design is never the winner.

\emph{The sequential design challenge is that the body is chosen before any search begins, so a better body can never be found, whatever controller is trained on the chosen one.} The thesis must therefore search over bodies as well as over controllers.

\section{The Morphology Optimization Challenge}
\label{sec:challenge-morphology}

The opposite of the sequential procedure is to fix the controller and search over the space of possible bodies. This is the cheap way to co-design. A single controller $\vect{\theta}_G$ is trained once, general enough to fly any body in the search space. The same controller then scores every candidate body, and the search maximizes $F(\vect{m}, \vect{\theta}_G)$ instead of $F(\vect{m}, \vect{\theta}^{\star}(\vect{m}))$. These two scores are different functions of $\vect{m}$. The difference between them is not a uniform offset or a uniform scaling, which a good optimizer could ignore. The difference can reverse the order of two bodies. Under the shared controller, the body $\vect{m}_1$ scores higher than the body $\vect{m}_2$. Under their own controllers, $\vect{m}_2$ scores higher than $\vect{m}_1$:
\begin{equation}
F(\vect{m}_1, \vect{\theta}_G) > F(\vect{m}_2, \vect{\theta}_G) \qquad \text{and} \qquad F(\vect{m}_1, \vect{\theta}^{\star}(\vect{m}_1)) < F(\vect{m}_2, \vect{\theta}^{\star}(\vect{m}_2)).
\label{eq:ranking-flip}
\end{equation}
In this case the search under $\vect{\theta}_G$ keeps $\vect{m}_1$ and discards $\vect{m}_2$, which is the body that would have been better.

There is a systematic reason to expect \eqref{eq:ranking-flip} to hold often rather than rarely. A generalist controller is trained on a whole distribution of bodies. Its weights must serve every body of that distribution, so on any single body they are a compromise. On any single body, the generalist therefore achieves less than a controller trained on that body alone. This gap is smallest near the centre of the training distribution. The gap grows towards the edges of the distribution, because there the dynamics of the body differ most from the average dynamics that the controller has learned to expect. The edges, however, are where the search wants to go. A body that is better than the average body of the distribution must differ from that average body: in size, in shape or in control authority. The more a body differs from the average body, the more it is penalized by a controller that was never adapted to it. The fixed controller thus does two things at once. It undervalues the bodies that differ most from its training distribution, which are exactly the bodies the search should find. And it rewards the bodies that it already flies well, which are the bodies close to the average one. A search under a fixed generalist is therefore biased towards the neighbourhood of the training distribution of that generalist. No amount of evolutionary pressure removes this bias, because the bias is built into the fitness function itself.

Training a controller after the search does not repair this bias. Suppose the search under $\vect{\theta}_G$ has returned its best body, and a controller is then trained on that body alone. The pair obtained is the best controller for the body that scored highest under the generalist. This pair is not the best pair either. Consider the body $\vect{m}_2$ of \eqref{eq:ranking-flip}: it scored lower than $\vect{m}_1$ under $\vect{\theta}_G$, but it gains more from a controller of its own. The search discarded $\vect{m}_2$, so no training afterwards can recover it. Specialization applied after the search cannot correct a search that did not account for specialization.

\emph{The morphology optimization challenge is that a search under one fixed controller does not find the bodies with the greatest potential, but the bodies that suit that controller.} What the search needs is a score that reflects what each body can do with a controller adapted to it. The next section shows why the direct way of obtaining such a score is too expensive.

\section{The Computational Feasibility Challenge}
\label{sec:challenge-feasibility}

The previous sections suggest that the co-design of body and controller is a promising direction for this thesis. The direct way to co-design is the one adopted by the co-design works that train their controllers by reinforcement learning \cite{gupta2021embodied, muff2026hexacopters}. These works solve the inner problem exactly: they train a controller from scratch for every body the search evaluates. The cost of this approach in the setting of this thesis can be estimated from three measured quantities. The first is the time needed to train one controller on many bodies. The second is the time needed to train one controller on a single body. The third is the time taken by one search over bodies in which every body is scored by flights alone, with no training inside the loop. \autoref{tab:feasibility} gives the figures.

A single training of the generalist controller takes fourteen hours. A single body search evaluates 1\,600 distinct bodies. Training a controller of that kind for each of them would take two and a half years on the same hardware. A specialist controller, trained on one body alone, is far cheaper: less than two hours on a single GPU. The specialist is the realistic form of the remedy. Even so, with two specialists trained at a time, one per GPU, one training per body brings the search to almost two months. The same search takes four days when every body is scored by flights alone. The factor between the two is almost fifteen at the present size of the search. And the size of the search is not fixed. Every additional body, generation or scenario multiplies the gap again.

The size of the search cannot be kept small. The performance of a body is a random quantity, for two reasons: the forests are generated at random, and the controller acts stochastically. A single flight therefore says little about a body. In this thesis each body is flown on sixty forests before its score is used, and even then the order of the bodies is far from noise-free. Whatever the cost of evaluating one body is, it is paid once per forest. It is paid again every time the controller changes, because the score of a body under an old controller is worthless once the controller has moved. A co-design loop whose inner level is a training run therefore cannot grow along any of the three axes the search needs: scenarios per body, bodies per generation and number of generations. The works that take this route stay under a thousand bodies in total \cite{muff2026hexacopters}, or require over a thousand processors for a single run \cite{gupta2021embodied}. Both works search far smaller design spaces than the one of this thesis, and both use far cheaper evaluations than the flight of a winged drone through a forest.

\emph{The computational feasibility challenge is that training a controller for every body the search visits costs more than any search over bodies can afford.} The feasibility challenge thus rules out the natural answer to the morphology optimization challenge. At the same time, it fixes the shape that any workable answer must have. The controller cannot be trained per body, so it must be trained once, for the whole space of bodies. A controller trained once is, by construction, not adapted to any one body. Its adaptation to the body being scored must therefore come from another mechanism, and that mechanism must cost no more than the flights that score the body anyway. Whether an adaptation that cheap can change which bodies the search finds is the empirical question of this thesis.
